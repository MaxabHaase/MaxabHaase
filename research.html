Research

Every chromosome must build exactly one functional centromere—the chromosomal site that assembles the kinetochore and connects DNA to the mitotic spindle. Yet centromeric DNA is among the most rapidly evolving parts of the genome. My research asks how such an essential molecular system can change so dramatically while continuing to transmit chromosomes accurately.

I combine comparative genomics, genetics, biochemistry, structural biology, and synthetic approaches to investigate two connected questions:

How do new centromere architectures evolve?

How does centromeric DNA direct the assembly of a functional kinetochore?

Yeasts provide an unusually powerful system for addressing these questions. Their centromeres span a remarkable range of architectures, from large repeat-rich regions to compact, genetically defined “point” centromeres. At the same time, many yeast species remain experimentally accessible but biologically unexplored. I use this diversity to discover natural experiments in chromosome biology and then reconstruct their underlying mechanisms.

The Evolutionary Origins and Remodeling of Centromeres

Point centromeres are among the most compact and genetically precise centromeres known. Despite being discovered more than forty years ago, their evolutionary origins remained obscure because they appeared to share little with the repeat-rich centromeres found in most eukaryotes.

Our recent work identified “proto-point” centromeres in relatives of brewer’s yeast. These centromeres retain features of repeat-rich ancestral centromeres while also possessing a single, sequence-positioned centromeric nucleosome. Comparative analyses indicated that point centromeres evolved through the co-option and progressive remodeling of Ty5 retrotransposon sequences.

This work has provided a launching point for investigating how mobile genetic elements, genome duplication, hybridization, and coevolution between centromeric DNA and kinetochore proteins reshape centromere architecture. I am particularly interested in how these forces drive transitions between compact and repeat-rich centromeres, and whether such transitions are constrained, predictable, or even reversible. More broadly, I want to understand how singular evolutionary events can redirect an entire chromosome-segregation system while preserving its essential function.

From a Single Kinetochore to the Architecture of Larger Centromeres

Most eukaryotic centromeres are large, repetitive regions that assemble multiple kinetochore–microtubule attachments on each chromosome. Human chromosomes, for example, typically form approximately twenty such attachments. In contrast, each Saccharomyces cerevisiae chromosome contains a single compact centromere that assembles one kinetochore and binds one microtubule.

I am studying the structure and assembly of native S. cerevisiae centromere–kinetochore complexes. Because this system contains only one centromeric nucleosome and one microtubule attachment, it provides an opportunity to define the fundamental structural unit from which larger regional centromeres may be built. Understanding this minimal unit could reveal how repeated kinetochore modules are organized and coordinated at the much larger centromeres found in humans and other eukaryotes.

The simplicity of the budding-yeast kinetochore is itself an evolutionary puzzle. The ancestors of point-centromere yeasts appear to have possessed larger centromeres with multiple centromeric nucleosomes and potentially multiple microtubule attachments. I therefore want to understand not only how the modern one-centromere–one-kinetochore–one-microtubule system works, but how it evolved from a larger ancestral architecture.

My current work combines native biochemical purification, chromatin reconstitution, cryo-electron microscopy, genetics, and comparative genomics. By integrating structure with evolutionary reconstruction, I aim to determine which features of kinetochore assembly represent ancient modular principles and which arose during the evolutionary reduction to a single attachment site.

Non-model Yeasts as Experimental Systems

Much of cell biology has been built from a small number of model organisms. Budding yeasts comprise more than a thousand described species, however, and encompass extensive variation in chromosome organization, cell-cycle regulation, genome content, and ecological specialization.

I use phylogenetics and comparative genomics to identify species in which evolution has naturally altered an otherwise conserved cellular process. These organisms can expose mechanisms that are difficult to recognize in conventional models and provide new systems for biochemical, genetic, and structural experiments.

My broader goal is to help establish the budding-yeast subphylum Saccharomycotina as a comparative model for mechanistic cell biology.

Other Efforts

Deep Mutational Scanning

During my PhD, I worked independently with the Bhabha and Ekiert laboratories at New York University to develop a pipeline for deep mutational scanning of bacterial phospholipid transport proteins, including MlaC and LetA. I have continued this work with Priyanka Verma at Washington University.

This work combines experimental selection, high-throughput sequencing, computational structural analysis, and protein biochemistry to map protein function at amino-acid resolution.
